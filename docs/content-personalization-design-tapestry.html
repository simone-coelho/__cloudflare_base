01 — THE PREMISE: ANONYMOUS FIRST

Built for the shoppers you've never seen.

We've adopted your name for this initiative deliberately: behavioral targeting and intelligence, not "site personalization." The distinction matters because of who your shoppers are. 90–95% of your traffic is anonymous. Roughly 80% has never been to the site before. Email — the identified channel — is about 10% of traffic. A system that depends on knowing who someone is will sit idle for nine out of ten visitors.

This design starts from the opposite premise: everything in it works from the first click of a first visit by a shopper you have never seen — using only what they do in the session, where they are, how they arrived, and what shoppers like them respond to. Warehouse data (Snowflake-derived analyses, for example) enriches the model as priors; it never becomes a dependency. And to put it in your own terms: product recommendations are table stakes — this initiative is content and experience recommendation.

Two commitments, up front

1 · Content recommendations here work the way product recommendations work — as a standalone serving capability. A content ID is treated like a product ID: registered, scored, ranked, served, measured. No experiment needs to exist for a decision to be made. Experimentation — including contextual bandits — remains available as an optional instrument you can point at any slot; it is never the engine and never a prerequisite.

2 · Your front end paints — always. We deliver decisions as data — IDs, order, scores, reasons — over a push contract, and your applications render. We never inject into or modify your pages.

Content isn't only a picture in a fixed box.

A piece of content can take several forms, and the catalog treats them as first-class types. A slot can hold any of them — and, as the gallery showed, a slot can hold a set whose internal order is itself personalized.

04 — HOW IT WORKS

One shopper's journey, in five steps.

  1. Your content is registered, labeled.

    Every image, banner, text block, and module gets an ID and simple labels — "evening," "lifestyle," "close-up," "reviews." This is the content catalog: the same idea as a product catalog, but for content. Where labels are sparse, AI proposes them and a person approves — a design-time enrichment step, never something running on the live site.

  2. A shopper browses, and the engine reads the signals.

    As they interact, the engine keeps a live interest thermometer: browsing evening styles makes the evening score rise; ignoring them a while lets it cool. Milliseconds, invisible, on every action. This is the affinity engine.

  3. Interests are matched to the content's labels.

    A high evening score means the evening-styled image outranks the everyday one — so that's the pick. Not a rule someone wrote: a live match between the shopper's behavior and the content's labels, recalculated every time.

  4. The decision is sent; the site displays it.

    A small message is pushed: "in this slot, show content #CMP1234, and here's why." The website does the actual rendering. Nothing is touched, redesigned, or injected into the page — the decision arrives as data.

  5. The system learns what works.

    Over time it observes which content leads to outcomes, for which kinds of shoppers, from which channels. Winners rise in the rankings — exactly how product recommendations improve — and every decision can answer "why did this shopper see this?" The full learning logic, in four layers, is set out below.

A real page is a wall of slots

The story followed one slot to keep it simple. A real product page might have eight sections — banner, gallery, story block, recommendations, reviews, styling module. Your template decides the slots; the engine chooses what fills each one, per shopper. One reading of the thermometer, several picks, sent together as one message. This multi-section delivery is proven technology — an existing deployment fills a ten-widget home page this way today, every widget resolved per request with duplicates removed and a fallback each. The new capability swaps a fixed-rule picker for live interest; the delivery shape is the same.

05 — THE MODEL, PRECISELY

Deterministic in computation, probabilistic in interpretation.

The five steps above are the story. This is the same machine written down precisely — the part built for your data science team to pressure-test. As summarized in our working session: a probabilistic model across a defined set of dimensions, with configurable weights and full visibility into those weights. That is the whole architecture; the rest of this document is the precision behind that sentence.

One clarification your data scientists will want first: the model is deterministic in computation and probabilistic in interpretation. Same inputs always produce the same scores — every number is reproducible and auditable end to end. The outputs are normalized affinities in [0, 1) that read as propensity estimates ("how likely is this shopper to respond to evening-styled content right now"). There is no black box anywhere in the serving path: no runtime model call, no unexplainable weights, no sampling noise.

Per-dimension scoring

Each dimension d (defined in the registry below) tracks a small vector of tag accumulators for each shopper — the interest thermometer you tapped through above, written as data. When a signal arrives for tag g at time t:

R[d,g] ← R[d,g] · e^(−Δt / τ_d) + w_action · s // decay the past, then add the new signal
  • Δt is the time since the accumulator was last touched; τ_d is the dimension's decay horizon — a tunable, short for volatile interests, long for stable taste.
  • w_action is the action's weight. Defaults: view 1 · save/wishlist 2 · add-to-cart 3 · purchase 5; for content interactions, impression 0.25 · click/tap 1 · dwell past threshold 2 · attributed conversion 5. All defaults, all tunable.
  • Decay is applied lazily at read time — state stores the value and its timestamp — which is mathematically identical to continuous decay. Interest cools during inactivity without any scheduled process.

Normalization — saturation

Raw accumulation is unbounded, so scores are normalized with a half-saturation constant:

a[d,g] = R[d,g] / (R[d,g] + K_d) // a ∈ [0,1) · a = 0.5 exactly when R = K_d

Early signals move the score fast; repeated identical signals show diminishing returns. K_d sets how much evidence "half-convinced" requires — per dimension, tunable.

Membership with hysteresis

Where affinities drive audience membership, entry and exit use two thresholds — defaults: enter at a ≥ 0.6, exit at a < 0.45 — so shoppers don't flap in and out at a boundary. Both thresholds are tunable per audience.

Session-over-session learning is the same math

A new session is just a large Δt: interests cool between visits at the dimension's own horizon rather than resetting. The shopper's state is durable — held at the edge, keyed to a stable first-party visitor ID, with every underlying event also recorded to the shopper's profile in ODP, the durable memory. If edge state is ever absent, it is re-seeded from ODP on the first event of the session. Long-horizon dimensions (category taste) persist across weeks; short-horizon dimensions (price sensitivity within a hunt) fade in minutes — because each dimension carries its own τ.

Behavioral dimensions are catalog-driven: their tag vocabularies come from your catalogs and content metadata, not from a hard-coded list — the same mechanism generates the dimension values for each brand from that brand's own data. That is also why the registry extends cleanly (see "Adding dimensions later," below).

Dimension 1 — Location, done as affinity, not as rules

The requirement is explicitly not "if Florida, don't show hoodies." Rule lists are brittle, manually maintained, and wrong at the edges. The version-one mechanism is affinity-driven at the population level:

  • Coarse geolocation on every request. The edge network supplies country / region / metro with the first request — no device permissions, no GPS, no PII, no third-party data. A brand-new visitor has a region before they have a click.
  • Regional engagement aggregates. For each region r and tag g, the system maintains decayed population accumulators P[r,g] fed by all shoppers' interactions in that region — the same decay mathematics as personal scoring, with a longer horizon (τ_region). From these, a regional lift: the share of engagement tag g gets in region r, relative to its global share. Lift > 1 means disproportionately loved there; lift < 1, disproportionately ignored. "Hoodies in Florida" demotes itself because Floridians don't engage with hoodies — the right behavior emerges from the data instead of being encoded by hand, and it stays current as tastes shift, automatically.

Cold-start blending. The affinity used for ranking blends personal signal with the regional prior:

ã = (1−λ) · a_personal + λ · â_regional // λ = K_blend / (K_blend + evidence)

λ shrinks as the shopper's own evidence accumulates. A first-time visitor with zero history gets a regionally-informed page on the first paint; within a handful of interactions, their own behavior takes over. This is the direct answer to the 80%-never-seen problem.

Tunables: regional granularity (country / state / metro), τ_region, K_blend, and a floor/ceiling on regional influence. All visible, all yours to adjust.

Dimension 2 — Visit number

Sessions are counted against a stable, device-scoped, first-party visitor ID — no fingerprinting; a new session begins after an inactivity gap, 30 minutes by default. The registry buckets visits (1 / 2–3 / 4+) and conditions content-type preference on the bucket — seeded with your merchandising knowledge as defaults (first product visit → on-model and lifestyle imagery; return visits → silo, detail/zoom, construction shots, fit and reviews), then reweighted from observed outcomes rather than left as assumptions. Your 40/60 split — 40% of purchases on visit one, 60% on subsequent visits — is exactly the pattern this dimension exists to exploit.

Identity honesty, stated plainly for your data scientists: a cleared cookie or a new device is a new visitor until an identifier exists to stitch on. When you later light up hashed-email capture on authenticated touchpoints, ODP stitches those histories server-side — the model needs no change; it simply inherits longer memories. Nothing in version one depends on that.

Dimension 3 — Entry / marketing channel

Classified at the edge, per session, from UTM parameters and referrer into an agreed taxonomy — proposed: paid social, paid search, organic search, email, direct, referral, affiliate. Channel acts two ways: as a context attribute conditioning outcome statistics (what converts for paid-social arrivals differs from search arrivals), and optionally as a strategy override (a slot may run a different weight profile for a given channel). You told us channel is your strongest single predictor; the registry treats it as first-class.

Dimension 4 — Content-type affinity

The engine can only serve the right form of asset if it knows the form. The content catalog types every asset against an agreed taxonomy — proposed starting point: on-model silo (product-only) detail/zoom construction/sole flat-lay video ugc editorial/story. The dimension then learns each shopper's response to each form — dwell, expansion, video completion — so a video-responsive shopper measurably accumulates video affinity and video assets rise in their rankings. This dimension is also what makes the visit-number defaults self-correcting: if a segment's return visitors actually prefer on-model imagery, the data overrides the seed.

Dimensions 5–8 — the behavioral core

These are the engine's native dimensions, running in production today in our live environment: product line/collection, silhouette/category, occasion, and price band — each with its own τ, K, and weights, each feeding both product-side and content-side scoring from one shopper vector. Price band deliberately runs a short horizon — price sensitivity is a state, not a trait.

Adding dimensions later

You raised that adding dimensions after launch can be hard — that is exactly why the registry is reviewed and agreed up front, and why it is versioned. Mechanically, the registry is configuration over a catalog-driven engine: adding a dimension is a registry change plus a decision about history. The new dimension learns from its activation forward, and where the underlying raw events were retained — they are, in ODP — it can be backfilled so it starts warm. What deserves scrutiny up front is not whether the machinery can add a dimension — it can — but whether the inputs for it exist: metadata, taxonomy, signals. That is what the workshop locks.

07 — STRATEGIES

A strategy is dimensions, weighted, applied to a slot.

The registry describes the shopper. A strategy decides how much each dimension matters, and where — it is the unit of control your team configures: a combination of dimensions, with weights, applied to a slot.

strategy = { slots: ["home-hero", "home-story-2"], // where it applies weights: { location: 1.0, visit: 0.8, channel: 0.6, content_type: 1.0, occasion: 0.7, … }, // the dimension mix constraints: { types: ["image","video"], excludeTags: […] }, // candidate rules mode: "configured" | "autonomous" }

At decision time, each candidate asset for the slot is scored: the shopper's (blended) affinities for the asset's tags, weighted by the strategy's dimension mix, plus the outcome-learning lift (set out below). Highest score wins the slot — and every decision records its arithmetic.

Two modes, both first-class:

  • Configured — your team sets the weights per slot (the homepage hero leans location + channel; a product-page gallery leans visit number + content-type). The tuning surface edits them live; changes take effect immediately, with no deployment.
  • Autonomous — the engine adjusts the weight mix itself from outcome statistics, with full visibility: every adjustment is logged, inspectable, and reversible, and any weight can be pinned. Autonomy is a dial per slot, not a philosophy.

Page assembly — the invariants

Unchanged from the contract we've already aligned on: one push per page carrying every slot's decision with an explicit order; no asset appears twice on a page; slots marked off-limits are never touched; any slot without a decision renders your default. The order field ships from day one — echoing your template now, so that when experience and layout personalization arrive later, reordering is a data change rather than a re-integration.

The delivery contract, literally

The strategy produces decisions; this is the payload that carries them — Shopper A's page from the walkthrough above, two of its slots shown, exactly as it arrives on the wire:

09 — THE LEARNING LOGIC

"How does it learn?" has four concrete answers.

Four layers, in increasing timescale, each named. None of them is a black box; none of them requires an experiment to be running.

L1 — Within the session (milliseconds)

The accumulate/decay mathematics above, applied on every event. This is where "she's hunting evening bags right now" comes from.

L2 — Across sessions

Shopper state is durable and decays on each dimension's own horizon; every event is also a fact on the ODP profile. A returning shopper's vector re-warms from durable memory at session start; the visit count increments. Interests cool between visits — they don't reset.

L3 — Outcome learning: what works

The engine accumulates per-item, context-conditioned outcome statistics over the open catalog: for each content item and context cell (channel × visit bucket × region, as agreed), a smoothed conversion lift versus that context's baseline — empirical-Bayes smoothing toward 1.0 with a configurable prior strength, so small samples don't produce wild swings. Ranking scores are multiplied by lift, so assets that demonstrably work for a context rise for that context.

New or under-observed content receives a configurable exploration share per slot (default 10%): a deterministic rotation guarantees fresh assets accumulate evidence instead of starving. Note what this is not: it is not variation buckets, and it is not bandit-dependent — it is item-level statistics over the whole catalog, the same way product recommendations learn. A contextual bandit (CMAB) remains available as an optional per-slot instrument if your team wants controlled variation testing on a specific placement; it is never required for the system to learn.

L4 — Discovery: what matters

Offline analysis over the accumulated decision-and-outcome corpus — cohort contrasts, predictive-attribute analysis per channel — surfaces proposals: "this behavior predicts conversion for paid-social visitors; want it as a dimension weight? as an audience?" Proposals are human-approved, never self-activating, and they arrive with the evidence that produced them. This is also where the registry grows over time, governed.

11 — HOW THIS DIFFERS FROM PRE-BUILT VARIATIONS

Ranking a catalog is not the same as choosing between pre-made versions.

A natural question is how this relates to the more familiar approach of building a few page versions and testing which performs best. They're genuinely different mechanisms. The kitchen analogy makes it click — tap each side.

Both have a place. Testing a few designed versions against each other is powerful when you know the versions you want to compare. Content personalization answers a different need: when you don't want to pre-decide the combinations, and would rather rank everything you have for each person — the way product recommendations already do.

The one-line distinction

Pre-built variations choose between a few designed versions; content personalization ranks everything in the catalog for each person. The bandit approach still has a role as one optional testing instrument inside the system — but it isn't the engine doing the picking.

12 — CONTROL & TRANSPARENCY

Every constant is a parameter. Every decision explains itself.

Everything so far describes what the system does. This section is about who holds the controls — three commitments, then the surfaces where your data scientists plug in.

  • Every constant in this document is a visible, editable parameter — action weights, decay horizons (τ), saturation constants (K), thresholds (θ), strategy weight profiles, blending constants, exploration share, smoothing priors. All of it is configuration, versioned, with change history; edits apply immediately, without a deployment.
  • Every decision explains itself. Each served decision carries its explain record: the dimensions that drove it, their scores and weights, the context, the lift applied. "Why did this shopper see this?" is a lookup, not an investigation.
  • No black box, structurally. There is no runtime model call to interrogate — the serving path is arithmetic you can recompute by hand from the explain record.

Injection surfaces — bring your own math

  1. Parameter control v1

    The full parameter surface above, via UI and API. This is not a demo toggle — it is the same configuration the engine reads.

  2. Priors import v1

    Seed the model with your own analyses: Snowflake-derived cohort priors, merchandising priors per visit bucket, regional seasonal priors. Imported priors enter as labeled prior mass and decay/blend under the same mathematics — your knowledge starts the model warm; behavior then confirms or corrects it.

  3. Full data egress — the closed loop v1

    Export every decision and outcome record, explain records included. Your team fits whatever they want offline — propensity models, uplift analyses — and writes the results back as priors or strategy weight profiles. Your math, our serving path, complete audit trail.

  4. Custom scoring hooks co-design

    A defined extension point for substituting or adjusting the ranking function on specific slots. We would rather design this with your data scientists against a real use case than guess at the interface — it is an explicit agenda item for the registry workshop.

18 — COMMON QUESTIONS

The things worth clarifying up front.

Does this build or change our website?

No. Decisions arrive as data; your site renders them. A slot with no decision shows your default, and a slot marked off-limits is never touched. It's designed to sit alongside your front end, not inside it.

Is it AI?

The per-shopper picking is transparent math — scores you can read and choices that explain themselves. AI assists in two supporting places: proposing content labels up front (human-approved), and explaining results in plain language through Opal. The decision path itself is deterministic.

Does it need to know who the shopper is?

No — the design is anonymous-first. Everything in it works from the first click of a first visit, using session behavior, region, and arrival channel; a brand-new visitor gets a regionally informed page on the first paint. Identity, where it later exists, only ever lengthens memory — it is never a prerequisite.

What do we need to have in place?

Your content (with access for the engine to register it), your ODP, and a website team willing to spend a workshop connecting the delivery kit and agreeing the slot taxonomy. That workshop is also where the ordering boundaries above get drawn.

Can we start small?

Yes — the foundation stage stands alone and deploys quickly, and content personalization can begin on a handful of slots on a single page type before widening. Nothing requires personalizing the whole site at once.

How do we know why a shopper saw something?

Every decision carries an explain record — the interests that drove it and the context that shaped it (channel, visit count, and so on). Opal is the surface that turns those records into plain-language answers and reporting.

Can our data scientists change the math?

Yes, through four surfaces: every parameter is live-editable — weights, decay horizons, thresholds, smoothing priors; your own analyses import as priors; every decision and outcome record exports for offline work and writes back as priors or weight profiles; and custom scoring hooks are an explicit co-design item. The detail is in the control section above.

What about the slots we never want personalized?

You mark them off-limits in configuration and they always render your default — header, footer, legal, or anything else you choose. This is enforced at assembly, so a personalized decision can never land in a slot you've excluded.

In one breath

Product recommendations pick the right products for each shopper. This does the same for content — the images, banners, and modules themselves — using the shopper's live behavior, on your own data, with every choice explainable — scored on an agreed, visible set of dimensions, and working from the first click for the shoppers you've never seen. The foundation is live, the delivery half is already shipped, and the brains half is demonstrated. This connects them.
